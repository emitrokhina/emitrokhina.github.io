About

I am a Ph.D. candidate in Political Science at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, specializing in comparative politics, political economy, and quantitative methods. I study state-society relations and political institutions in authoritarian regimes, especially how governments use access to information laws, courts, and administrative transparency as instruments of control rather than channels of citizen oversight.

Methodologically, I pair original administrative and judicial data (including a hand-built corpus of roughly 30,000 access to information court cases in Russia) with quasi-experimental and survey-experimental designs. My dissertation, Transparency Defanged: How Autocrats Enforce Freedom of Information, uses these tools to explain when transparency from above fails to empower citizens.

Research

Transparency as Control: Freedom of Information Litigation in Authoritarian Russia

Dissertation chapter · Work in progress

Access-to-information laws are understood as instruments of accountability, yet authoritarian states adopt and actively administer them. Drawing on a census of roughly 30,000 court decisions citing Russia’s access-to-information statute, I show how litigation becomes a venue in which the state manages rather than concedes its own transparency.

Transparency under Constraint: The Adoption of Access-to-Information Laws in Autocracies

Working paper

Why do authoritarian regimes adopt transparency laws, and what shapes the design of the laws they produce? I argue that formally credible but weakly enforceable legislation becomes viable only when external reputational incentives make adoption valuable as a governance signal and the regime already controls the institutions that would enforce it. I trace five legislative attempts in Russia between 1987 and 2009.

Constraining the Public Record: Investigative Media and Administrative Transparency in Authoritarian Russia

With Malika Talgatova · Work in progress

Does investigative reporting that leans on official sources lead agencies to disclose less? Combining more than 750,000 Russian independent-media publications (2011–2022) with annual openness scores for 71 federal executive agencies, we use event-study designs to test whether agencies retrench after being cited as evidence.

The Politics of Inaction

With Kathryn Hendley · Work in progress

A survey experiment on why citizens so rarely exercise transparency rights that formally exist, distinguishing constraints of awareness, expected efficacy, and fear.
